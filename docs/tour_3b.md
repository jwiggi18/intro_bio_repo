### Scene 2: Osmosis

**☐ CAM 29** | draws: water molecule, then a membrane with more solute on one side

One more, and it's the one that matters most for what's coming. Water.

**☐ CAM 29** | draws: labels, "SOLUTE, the stuff dissolved" and "SOLVENT, what it's dissolved in"

Two quick words. The solute is the stuff dissolved. The solvent is what it's dissolved in. In your body, the solvent is basically always water.

**☐ CAM 30** | draws: two sides, one crowded with solute, showing less room for water

Here's the thing to see. Where there's more solute dissolved, there's proportionally less free water. More salt in the same space means less room for water in that space.

**☐ CAM 30** | draws: water moving from the dilute side to the concentrated side *(GIF)*

And water diffuses, just like everything else. It moves from where there's more of it to where there's less of it.

**☐ CAM 31** | draws: label, "OSMOSIS"

That's osmosis. That's the whole thing. Water is not attracted to salt. Water is not chasing anything. Water is diffusing down its own gradient, following the exact same rule the food coloring followed. We give it its own name because it matters so much, not because it works differently.

**☐ CAM 32** | draws: three cells, shriveled, normal, swollen

And now the vocabulary. If the fluid outside a cell has more solute than the inside, water leaves and the cell shrinks. We call that solution hypertonic. If the outside has less solute, water moves in and the cell swells. That's hypotonic. If they're equal, no net movement, and that's isotonic.

**☐ CAM 32** | draws: the three labels held

Keep the picture, not the prefix. The prefix is just a label for something you can already see.

### Scene 3: Aquaporins

**☐ CAM 33** | draws: bare bilayer, water trickling across slowly

One loose end. Water gets across a bare membrane on its own, but slowly. For most cells, slow is fine.

**☐ CAM 33** | draws: a kidney, "180 liters a day"

But some cells move staggering amounts of water. Your kidneys filter and reabsorb something like 180 liters a day. Slow does not work there.

**☐ CAM 34** | draws: aquaporin, water passing single file, fast *(GIF)*

So those cells build dedicated water channels called aquaporins. A narrow pore that takes water single file, millions of molecules a second, and lets almost nothing else through.

**☐ CAM 34** | draws: "Peter Agre, Nobel Prize, 2003"

Peter Agre won a Nobel Prize for finding these in 2003, which is remarkably recent for something this basic to how you work.

**☐ CAM 34** | draws: arrow forward

Remember aquaporins. They're going to matter more than you expect.
