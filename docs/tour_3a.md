### Scene 3: Two jobs

**☐ CAM 10** | draws: clean membrane, centered

Here's how I want you to think about all of this. Everything embedded in that membrane is doing one of two jobs. Just two.

**☐ CAM 10** | draws: "1. TRANSPORT," a molecule crossing

The first job is transport. Things have to physically get across.

**☐ CAM 10** | draws: "2. COMMUNICATION," a molecule binding the surface

The second job is communication. The cell has to be able to detect what's around it, and other cells have to be able to detect the cell.

**☐ CAM 11** | draws: both labels held together, wide shot

Transport and communication. We're doing transport in this video. Communication gets its own.

### Scene 4: Two kinds of transport

**☐ CAM 12** | draws: a ball rolling down a hill

Transport comes in two kinds, and the difference is who pays.

**☐ CAM 12** | draws: label "PASSIVE, downhill, free"

Passive transport is downhill. Things move from where there's more of them to where there's less of them, and the cell doesn't spend any energy making that happen. It's free.

**☐ CAM 13** | draws: a ball being pushed up a hill, ATP burning

Active transport is uphill. The cell moves something from where there's less of it to where there's more of it, and that costs energy every single time.

**☐ CAM 13** | draws: "ACTIVE" gets a box around it, set aside

We're going to spend a whole video on active transport, because the reason a cell would ever pay for that is one of the most important ideas in this course. Today is passive.

### Scene 5: Diffusion

**☐ CAM 14** | draws: a drop of food coloring in a glass of still water

Let's start with what passive transport actually is. Drop food coloring into a glass of water and don't stir it.

**☐ CAM 14** | draws: the color spreading out over time *(GIF)*

Over time, it spreads out until the color is even everywhere.

**☐ CAM 15** | draws: individual dye molecules bouncing randomly

Nothing pushed it. Every one of those molecules is just moving randomly, bumping around. But when you have a crowd on one side and empty space on the other, random motion moves more of them out than back.

**☐ CAM 15** | draws: net movement arrow, crowd to empty

Not because they're going anywhere on purpose. Because there are more of them here than there. That's diffusion.

### Scene 6: Concentration gradients

**☐ CAM 16** | draws: gradient bar, high on one side, low on the other

That difference, more here and less there, is called a concentration gradient. And the gradient is what makes things move.

**☐ CAM 16** | draws: an open channel with nothing moving through it

An open door doesn't make anything walk through it. The difference across the door is what does that.

**☐ CAM 17** | draws: text on screen, held

Now here's something I want you to notice, and I want you to keep noticing it for the rest of this course.

**Diffusion moves things toward balance. Life moves things away from it.**

**☐ CAM 17** | draws: a cell holding a steep gradient, labeled "built and held"

Diffusion, left alone, always evens things out. But living cells spend enormous energy keeping things uneven. Your cells are not trying to reach balance. They are working, constantly, to avoid it.

**☐ CAM 17** | draws: arrow pointing forward, "active transport"

That's what the active transport video is about. Hold onto it.

### Scene 7: Passive transport, three ways

**☐ CAM 18** | draws: three empty panels appearing side by side

Passive transport happens three different ways, and which one you get depends on the molecule.

### Scene 8: Simple diffusion

**☐ CAM 19** | draws: O₂ and CO₂ crossing the bare bilayer, no protein *(GIF)*

The first one we've already seen. If a molecule has no charge and it isn't polar, it just goes through the membrane on its own. Oxygen. Carbon dioxide. No protein involved at all.

**☐ CAM 19** | draws: label, "SIMPLE DIFFUSION"

That's simple diffusion. Simple because nothing helps.

### Scene 9: Channel-facilitated diffusion

**☐ CAM 20** | PAN BACK to Na⁺ in its sphere of hydration, then bring it to the membrane

Now sodium. Sodium is charged, and it's wearing that sphere of hydration, and the insulated center of the membrane rejects it.

**☐ CAM 20** | draws: Na⁺ bouncing off the bare bilayer *(GIF)*

On its own, sodium is not getting across. It needs a door.

**☐ CAM 21** | draws: a channel protein spanning the membrane

That door is a channel protein. It goes all the way through, from outside to inside.

**☐ CAM 21** | draws: zoom on the lining, charges marked along the inside

And the inside of that channel is lined with charges. That gives the ion something to hold onto the whole way across.

**☐ CAM 22** | draws: Na⁺ entering, hydration sphere releasing, ion passing, water re-forming *(GIF)*

It lets go of its sphere of hydration and grabs the lining of the channel instead. Then it picks the water back up on the other side. It's a trade, not a sacrifice.

**☐ CAM 23** | draws: a second channel turning a different ion away

Channels are picky. Really picky. A channel that lets sodium through will turn other ions away, even ones that look almost the same.

**☐ CAM 23** | draws: label, "CHANNEL-FACILITATED DIFFUSION"

That's channel-facilitated diffusion. Still passive, still downhill, still free. The channel isn't pushing. It's just making the trip possible.

### Scene 10: Carrier-facilitated diffusion

**☐ CAM 24** | draws: glucose molecule approaching, OH groups marked, "POLAR"

Now here's the one that surprises people. Glucose. Sugar. Glucose is polar, the same way water is polar. It's covered in little polar bonds. And like water, it has no charge on it.

**☐ CAM 25** | draws: water trickling through slowly beside glucose bouncing off

Remember what water does. Water is polar, and it does get across, just slowly, because water is very small.

**☐ CAM 25** | draws: size comparison, glucose dwarfing water

Glucose is polar too, but look at the size of it. Being polar slows you down. Being polar and big stops you.

**☐ CAM 26** | draws: glucose held against a channel opening, doesn't fit

It doesn't fit through a channel either. And that's not about how big the protein is, it's about how narrow the opening is. Channels have very narrow openings, and that narrowness is exactly what makes them picky. Glucose is about three times too wide.

**☐ CAM 26** | draws: a cartoonishly wide channel, everything pouring through both ways

And a cell can't just build a wider one. A hole that big would let almost anything through, in both directions, all the time. You'd lose the barrier you just built.

**☐ CAM 27** | draws: carrier protein, three states, open-out / closed with cargo / open-in *(GIF)*

Glucose needs something different. Not a hole at all. A protein that grabs it on one side, closes around it, and opens on the other side. It's never a tunnel.

**☐ CAM 28** | draws: channel labeled "door," carrier labeled "revolving door"

That's a carrier. A channel is a door. A carrier is more like a revolving door. Something has to physically move.

**☐ CAM 28** | draws: label, "CARRIER-FACILITATED DIFFUSION"

And this is still passive. The carrier isn't pushing glucose anywhere. Glucose is still going downhill. The carrier just makes the trip possible.

**☐ CAM 29** | WIDE shot, pulling back to show all three types at once
