## Week 5: DNA and Genetic Variation (LO4)

**From Unit 1 top-level outcomes:**
- Describe DNA structure and function, including nucleotide components, the sugar-phosphate backbone, base pairing rules, and how DNA encodes information.
- Describe the role of mutations as the source of genetic variation and explain why this matters for evolution.

**From Unit 1, "Molecular Genetics" (detailed breakdown):**
- Identify the components of a nucleotide and explain which parts form the DNA backbone and base pairs.
- Distinguish between purines and pyrimidines and describe how they pair in DNA.
- Explain that mutations are the ultimate source of genetic variation on Earth.

## Week 6: Gene Expression (LO4)

**From Unit 1 top-level outcomes:**
- Explain how genetic information flows from DNA to protein, including transcription, translation, codons, amino acids, and the effects of mutations (synonymous, nonsynonymous, and frameshift).

**From Unit 1, "Molecular Genetics" (detailed breakdown):**
- Determine an mRNA transcript sequence from a DNA coding or template strand.
- Explain the role of codons in translation and why they matter for protein synthesis.
- Differentiate between synonymous, nonsynonymous, and frameshift mutations, and explain why frameshifts are often especially disruptive.
- Describe the relationship between translation, proteins, and amino acids.

## Week 7: Cell Division (LO5)

**From Unit 1 top-level outcomes:**
- Compare mitosis and meiosis, identifying how each process contributes to growth, repair, reproduction, and genetic diversity.
- Distinguish between homologous chromosomes and sister chromatids, and explain how meiosis generates haploid gametes through segregation, recombination, and independent assortment.

**From Unit 1, "Cell Division & Genetics" (detailed breakdown):**
- Compare the outcomes of mitosis and meiosis.
- Distinguish between homologous chromosomes and sister chromatids.
- Explain why meiosis I produces haploid cells and how meiosis results in four unique haploid gametes.
- Describe how recombination and independent assortment contribute to genetic diversity.
- Explain how meiosis illustrates Mendel's laws of segregation and independent assortment.
- Describe the evolutionary importance of meiosis.
- Explain unique features of meiosis in egg production, including cell cycle arrest and fertilization-dependent completion.

## Week 8: Inheritance (LO5)

**From Unit 1 top-level outcomes:**
- Apply principles of inheritance (Mendel's laws, chromosomal linkage, monogenic and polygenic traits) to explain patterns of genetic variation and their evolutionary implications.

**From Unit 1, "Cell Division & Genetics" (detailed breakdown):**
- Differentiate between monogenic and polygenic traits and explain how chromosomal linkage affects inheritance.
- Be able to identify and explain extensions of Mendelian genetics and their mechanisms (e.g. Incomplete and Co-Dominance, Sex-linked inheritance).

## Week 9: Evolution and Natural Selection (LO6)

**From Unit 2, "Evolution":**
- Identify common misconceptions about evolution and articulate why they are inaccurate. / Identify and correct common misconceptions (e.g., "evolution is just a theory," "individuals evolve," "evolution has a goal").
- Explain mutation as the source of genetic variation and evaluate why variation is essential for evolutionary processes. / Explain mutation as the ultimate source of variation and evaluate its role in adaptation.
- Explain how meiosis and sexual reproduction remix alleles and analyze how this contributes to population-level diversity. / Analyze how meiosis and sexual reproduction remix alleles (segregation, recombination, independent assortment) to generate genetic diversity.
- Compare natural selection, sexual selection, and genetic drift, and analyze how each drives evolutionary change under different conditions. / Compare natural selection, sexual selection, and genetic drift; analyze contexts in which each predominates.

## Week 11: Cellular Respiration (LO7)

**From Unit 4, "Cellular Respiration" (full section, direct match):**
- Explain why organisms need ATP and analyze why cellular respiration is essential for sustaining life.
- Describe how energy stored in food molecules becomes usable energy for cellular work.
- Analyze how cellular respiration connects to energy flow within ecosystems.
- Describe the overall purpose and chemical equation of cellular respiration.
- Identify whether a given biological scenario represents aerobic or anaerobic respiration based on oxygen availability and organismal context.
- Identify the major stages of cellular respiration (glycolysis, pyruvate oxidation, Krebs cycle, electron transport chain) and the cellular locations where they occur.
- Trace the flow of energy from glucose to ATP through oxidation reactions, electron carriers (NADH and FADH₂), and ATP synthase.
- Explain how the electron transport chain generates a proton gradient and how ATP synthase uses this gradient to produce ATP.
- Compare the ATP yield of aerobic and anaerobic respiration.
- Predict how changes in oxygen levels, mitochondrial function, or enzyme activity would alter ATP production.
- Analyze how cellular respiration and photosynthesis together drive global energy flow.

**Detailed breakdown, also from Unit 4:**
- Describe how glycolysis breaks down glucose and identify its ATP and NADH outputs.
- Explain the formation of acetyl-CoA during pyruvate oxidation.
- Trace carbon through the Krebs cycle and identify the outputs.
- Describe how electron transport and chemiosmosis generate most cellular ATP.
- Identify conditions under which organisms rely on anaerobic respiration (e.g., oxygen-poor environments, rapid ATP demand).
- Predict how inhibitors of the electron transport chain (e.g., cyanide) disrupt ATP production.
- Explain how carbon released in respiration contributes to the carbon cycle.

## Week 12: Photosynthesis (LO7)

**From Unit 3, "Photosynthesis" (full section, direct match):**
- Describe the overall purpose and chemical equation for photosynthesis.
- Describe the structure of chloroplasts and explain how this structure supports photosynthetic function.
- Explain that photons are packets of light energy, describe the relationship between wavelength and energy, and analyze which wavelengths are absorbed or reflected when we perceive color.
- Distinguish between the light-dependent reactions (in the thylakoid membranes) and the Calvin cycle (in the stroma).
- Trace the movement of energy from light absorption to the formation of glucose.
- Explain how electron transport, H⁺ gradients, and ATP synthase generate chemical energy.
- Describe how NADPH and ATP from the light reactions power carbon fixation in the Calvin cycle.
- Predict how environmental conditions (light intensity, CO₂ levels, temperature) influence photosynthetic efficiency.

## Week 13: Biological Systems (LO8)

**From Unit 2, "Nervous System" (full section):**
- Describe the structure and function of a typical neuron, including the role of the axon hillock in signal integration.
- Differentiate graded potentials from action potentials by analyzing how each is generated, propagated, and integrated.
- Explain how the Na⁺/K⁺ pump maintains resting potential and predict the effects of pump inhibition on neuronal signaling.
- Trace the sequence of events during an action potential from initiation to neurotransmitter release at the axon terminal.
- Analyze what happens to neurotransmitters after release, including receptor binding, reuptake, and enzymatic degradation.
- Trace the pathway of a reflex arc and evaluate its significance for rapid response.
- Identify the major divisions of the nervous system and analyze how they coordinate function.
- Analyze how selected infections and diseases alter normal nervous system function.

**From Unit 3, "Immune System" (full section):**
- Identify major leukocytes discussed in lecture, describe their functions, and classify them as part of the innate or adaptive immune system.
- Distinguish between the barrier and internal divisions of the innate immune system and describe their major components.
- Trace the sequence of events in the immune response to a primary infection and compare it with the secondary immune response.
- Explain how vaccines influence immune response speed and strength.
- Describe how B cells generate antibody diversity through V(D)J recombination and become more specific after first antigen exposure through somatic hypermutation.
- Explain the functions of antibodies and describe one major function of the complement system (formation of pores in pathogens and infected cells).

**Gaps:** no existing outcome covers the endocrine system (hormones, target cells, feedback loops; Sections 37.1-37.2 are assigned readings with nothing written yet) or general animal body organization/homeostasis (Sections 33.1, 33.3). This week is carrying three sub-topics (body organization, nervous, endocrine) plus immune/disease, and only two of the four have existing outcomes.

## Week 14: Ecosystems (LO9)

**From Unit 3, "Population Ecology" (full section):**
- Compare r-selected and K-selected species and evaluate how life-history strategies influence survival and reproduction.
- Interpret and construct survival curves (Type I, II, III) and analyze what they reveal about species' ecological strategies.
- Analyze how population growth patterns (exponential vs. logistic) relate to carrying capacity and environmental limitations.
- Evaluate how natural selection and life-history trade-offs shape r/K strategies and survival patterns.

**From Unit 4, "Ecosystems: Food Webs, Energy Flow & Biogeochemical Cycles" (full section):**
- Explain why energy flow and nutrient cycling are essential for ecosystem stability.
- Analyze how disruptions to energy flow or nutrient cycles affect biodiversity and ecosystem function.
- Explain why ecosystems require continual energy input but can recycle matter indefinitely.
- Interpret food chains and food webs, identifying producers, consumers, decomposers, and detritivores.
- Apply the 10% rule to calculate energy transfer among trophic levels and analyze limitations on higher trophic levels.
- Evaluate how changes in one species can affect the stability of food webs through direct and indirect interactions.
- Describe the major reservoirs, pathways, and biological significance of the carbon, nitrogen, and phosphorus cycles.
- Explain that atmospheric nitrogen (N₂) cannot be used directly by most organisms and must be fixed by soil bacteria before entering food webs.
- Predict how human activities influence biogeochemical cycles and assess resulting ecological impacts.

*This is the best-covered week in the whole mapping: population ecology and ecosystems outcomes both land here cleanly, and the last bullet above (human impacts on biogeochemical cycles) is worth noting since it overlaps with Week 15's theme.*
