## Voice notes

Drawn from `Cell_membrane_intro-English.txt`. **Important distinction:** a transcript
captures disfluency as well as style. Only the second is worth writing into a script.

### Keep. This is the teaching voice.

- **Restate the idea a second way** right after saying it. Say it, then say it differently.
  This is the single biggest difference from the early drafts, which compress instead
- **Narrate the visual out loud.** "If we look at this visual, the red is the oxygen"
- **Plain words, not literary ones.** No "water crowds it into a corner"
- **Ask a question, then answer it.** "What's happening when it dissolves?"
- **"Remember"** callbacks to earlier material
- Flag importance directly: "and again, this is really important"
- Occasional warm aside ("this is what makes things yummy, right")
- End pointing forward: "and we'll talk about that next"
- **No dramatic one-line paragraphs for effect.** That is a Claude tic, not Jodie's
- Keep **"insulator"** for the hydrophobic core and **"selectively permeable"**

### Reduce. This is speech habit, not style.

- **"So" as a sentence opener.** Jodie is actively working to drop this. Target one or
  two per video, maximum
- **"Okay." as a reset beat.** Same. Currently zero across all five scripts
- Self-corrections and restarts

**"So" meaning "therefore" is fine and stays.** "It's polar, so it gets rejected" is a
logical connector doing real work. "So. Here's the next thing" is filler. The four
remaining instances across all five scripts are all the first kind.

**What replaces the filler:** the transitions that used to lean on "So" and "Okay" now
carry actual content. A question, a direct address, a pointer to what is on screen, or
the structure itself ("That's matter. Now job two."). Deleting filler without replacing
it makes a script read clipped, which pushes back toward the over-polished register.

# Video 1: Why Is There an Inside?

**1,098 words ≈ 8 min** | Opens the arc

### Scene 1: The hook

*[Visual: title card, then a person outline losing water, then the WHO map of active outbreaks]*

There's a disease that can take a healthy adult and kill them in a day. Not through fever, not through a wound. Through water.

Cholera starts when someone drinks contaminated water. Within hours, they begin losing fluid at a rate that's hard to believe. Up to a liter an hour. Not for one hour, for hours. A person can lose more than a tenth of their body weight in a day. Blood pressure drops. If you pinch the skin, it stays pinched, because there isn't enough water left in the tissue for it to spring back. Untreated, somewhere between thirty and fifty percent of severe cases die.

And this isn't history. Between January and late October of 2025, thirty-two countries reported more than 565,000 cases and over 7,000 deaths. Hold onto one detail there: those are the *reported* numbers. The real total is higher, because most cholera happens in places where surveillance is thinnest. Reported and actual are two different things, and knowing the difference between them is most of what it means to read science well.

### Scene 2: The turn

*[Visual: zoom from the person, to the gut, to a single cell, to the membrane]*

Now here's the part I want you to sit with. Everything I just described happens because of one protein, in one membrane, getting stuck in the wrong position.

That's the whole disease. One channel, jammed open.

Before we can explain cholera, we have to talk about the membrane. And to talk about the membrane, we have to start somewhere that might feel like a detour. Stay with me, because it isn't one.

### Scene 3: The membrane, zoomed in

*[Visual: simple phospholipid bilayer, wide shot. Inside labeled, outside labeled.]*

This is a cell membrane. The single most important thing it does is right here in the picture: it separates the inside of the cell from the outside of the cell. That's it. That's the job.

Let's zoom in on a small section of it.

*[Visual: zoom to a few phospholipid molecules]*

The membrane is made of phospholipids. And that matters, but it doesn't matter yet, because to see why it matters you need to know something about water.

### Scene 4: Water and polar covalent bonds

*[Visual: build a water molecule. Oxygen, two hydrogens, bent shape. Then shade the charges.]*

Living things are made mostly of water. So whatever a cell does, it does it in water.

A water molecule is one oxygen bonded to two hydrogens, and those are polar covalent bonds. Covalent means the electrons are shared. Polar means they're shared unevenly. The electrons spend more of their time with the oxygen than with the hydrogens.

*[Visual: δ− appears on oxygen, δ+ on each hydrogen]*

So the oxygen ends up slightly negatively charged, and the hydrogens end up slightly positively charged. Not fully charged. Slightly.

### Scene 5: Hydrogen bonds

*[Visual: several water molecules linking up, positive to negative, in a shifting network]*

Those slight charges let water molecules stick to each other. The slightly positive hydrogen of one molecule is attracted to the slightly negative oxygen of the next. That attraction is a hydrogen bond.

Any single hydrogen bond is weak. But there are a lot of them, and together they're strong enough that you've watched them work. This is why water climbs a napkin. It's why water climbs a straw. The molecules are holding onto each other and pulling each other along.

### Scene 6: Ionic bonds and spheres of hydration

*[Visual: sodium atom and chlorine atom, electron transfers, then the crystal]*

Water isn't the only thing in nature carrying charge, and that's where this gets useful.

Take table salt. Sodium and chlorine are held together by an ionic bond, and an ionic bond is different. The electrons aren't shared at all. Sodium's outer electron leaves and moves onto the chlorine.

*[Visual: superscripts appear. Na⁺ and Cl⁻]*

From here on, you'll see a little plus on the sodium and a little minus on the chlorine. Those aren't decoration. The plus means that atom gave an electron away. The minus means that one took it. And notice the name changed: chlorine became chlor*ide*. That "-ide" ending is how we mark the negative ion.

Now drop that salt in water.

*[Visual: crystal pulls apart, water molecules swarm and orient around each ion]*

Water pulls the crystal apart. Then it wraps each ion individually. The negative oxygen ends turn toward the positive sodium. The positive hydrogen ends turn toward the negative chloride. Each ion ends up surrounded by its own shell of water molecules, all facing the right direction.

That shell has a name. It's called a **sphere of hydration**, and I need you to remember it. It's going to come back and explain something important.

### Scene 7: So why does any of this matter to membranes?

*[Visual: back out to the bilayer]*

But we were talking about cell membranes. Why does all of this matter to them?

Because cell membranes are made mostly of lipids. And lipids are made mostly of carbon and hydrogen, which share their electrons just about equally. No greedy end. No slight charges. Lipids are nonpolar.

Which means water has nothing to interact with. Nothing to grab, nothing to hold hands with. And remember, living things are mostly water. The cells these membranes define are sitting in a water-rich environment, inside and out.

### Scene 8: So what?

*[Visual: oil and vinegar separating in a jar]*

So water doesn't want to interact with lipids. What does that get us?

Here's what. It isn't that lipids are attracted to each other. It's that water molecules are so attracted to each other that they push out anything that can't participate. Oil doesn't gather together because oil is sociable. Water crowds it into a corner.

And the membrane isn't just lipids. It's *phospho*lipids, and that difference is central to how cell membranes, and therefore cells, exist at all.

### Scene 9: Phospholipids

*[Visual: one phospholipid, head and two tails, color-coded]*

A phospholipid is two incompatible pieces bolted together. Two fatty acid tails that are nonpolar, that water pushes away. And a phosphate head that's charged, that water pulls in.

One molecule, both problems. The word for that is **amphipathic**, from Greek: *amphi*, meaning both, and *pathos*, meaning feeling. Both-feeling.

*[Visual: scattered phospholipids in water, then self-organizing into a bilayer]*

Put a pile of them in water and ask what happens. The heads want in. The tails have to go somewhere else. Neither end gets to win, so they settle into the only arrangement that satisfies both at once. Heads out toward the water on both sides. Tails tucked inward, facing each other.

Two sheets. A bilayer.

And nobody built that. There's no machine assembling it, no instructions, no energy spent. Water's need to hold onto itself is enough to organize the entire structure.

### Scene 10: The sheet closes

*[Visual: flat sheet with exposed tails at the edge, glowing red. Sheet curls, edges meet, seals into a sphere.]*

But look at a flat sheet. It has an edge, and at that edge the tails are exposed to water again. The edge is still a problem.

So the sheet curls. And it keeps curling until the edges meet and seal.

The moment that happens, there's an inside and an outside. Not because anything set out to build a container, but because a sheet with an edge is unstable, and closing is the only way out of it.

That's where we started. The membrane separates the inside of the cell from the outside. Now you know why there's an inside at all. Water wouldn't tolerate an edge.

### Scene 11: Bridge

Next time we'll look at the problem we just created. Because that barrier is very good at keeping out exactly the things a cell needs most.

# Video 3: Why Anything Moves

**660 words ≈ 5 min**

### Scene 1: The question

*[Visual: a channel sitting open in the membrane, nothing moving through it]*

We've got a membrane, and we've got channels in it. Open doors.

But an open door doesn't make anything walk through it. Why would sodium move into or out of a cell at all? What actually makes it go?

### Scene 2: Diffusion

*[Visual: dye dropped into still water, spreading out over time]*

Start with something simpler. Drop food coloring into a glass of water and don't stir it. Over time it spreads out until the color is even everywhere.

Nothing pushed it. Nothing pulled it. Every one of those dye molecules is moving randomly, constantly, bumping around. And when you have a crowd on one side and empty space on the other, random motion moves more of them out than back. Not because they're going anywhere on purpose. Because there are more of them here than there.

That's diffusion. Things spread from where there are a lot of them to where there are fewer, until it evens out.

### Scene 3: Concentration gradients, and the line that matters

*[Visual: gradient bar, high concentration one side, low the other, arrow showing net movement]*

That difference, more here and less there, is a concentration gradient. And a gradient is what makes things move. Not the open door. The difference across it.

*[Visual: the sentence on screen, held]*

Now here's something I want you to notice, and I want you to keep noticing it for the rest of this course.

**Diffusion moves things toward balance. Life moves things away from it.**

Diffusion, left alone, always evens things out. But living cells spend an enormous amount of energy keeping things uneven. Your cells are not trying to reach balance. They're working, constantly, to avoid it. We'll get to why in the next video, but plant that now, because almost every system in your body runs on differences that your cells paid to create.

### Scene 4: Osmosis is not a new rule

*[Visual: U-tube, membrane in the middle, more solute on one side]*

Now let's do water, because water is where students usually get told there's a special rule. There isn't one.

First, two words. The **solute** is the stuff dissolved. The **solvent** is what it's dissolved in. In your body, the solvent is basically always water.

Here's the thing to see. Where there's more solute dissolved, there's proportionally less free water. More salt in the same space means less room for water in that space. If one side has more solute, that side has less water.

*[Visual: water molecules moving from the dilute side to the concentrated side]*

And water diffuses, just like everything else. It moves from where there's more of it to where there's less of it.

That's osmosis. That's the whole thing. Water is not attracted to salt. Water is not chasing anything. Water is diffusing down its own gradient, following the exact same rule the food coloring followed. We give it a separate name because it matters so much, not because it works differently.

### Scene 5: The vocabulary

*[Visual: three cells. Shriveled, normal, swollen.]*

Now that you can picture it, here are the words for it.

If the fluid outside a cell has more solute than the inside, water leaves, and the cell shrinks. We call that solution hypertonic. If the outside has less solute, water moves in, and the cell swells. That's hypotonic. If they're equal, there's no net movement, and that's isotonic.

Keep the picture, not the prefix. The prefix is just a label for what you already understand.

### Scene 6: Aquaporins

*[Visual: bare bilayer with water trickling, then an aquaporin with water in single file, fast]*

One loose end. Remember that water gets across a bare membrane on its own, but slowly?

For most cells, slow is fine. But some cells move staggering amounts of water. Your kidneys filter and reabsorb something on the order of 180 liters a day. Slow does not work there.

So those cells build dedicated water channels called aquaporins. A narrow pore that takes water molecules single file, millions per second, and lets almost nothing else through. Peter Agre won a Nobel Prize for finding them in 2003, which is remarkably recent for something this basic to how you work.

Remember aquaporins. They're going to matter more than you'd expect.

### Scene 7: Bridge

So things move because of gradients, and water moves for exactly the same reason everything else does.

Which leaves the question I skipped. If diffusion always evens things out, and your cells are full of differences that haven't evened out, then somebody is paying for that.

Who?

# Video 4: Who's Paying

**480 words ≈ 3.5 min** | Short video, big idea

### Scene 1: The setup

*[Visual: a cell with steep gradients shown across its membrane, holding steady]*

Last video ended with a problem. Diffusion evens things out. Always. Given enough time and no interference, every gradient collapses.

And yet your cells are full of gradients that have not collapsed. There's far more sodium outside your cells than inside. Far more potassium inside than out. Those differences are still there right now, and they've been there your whole life.

So something is holding them there. Something is paying.

### Scene 2: Channels versus pumps

*[Visual: side by side. A channel, ion falling through. A pump, ion being pushed the other way, ATP shown.]*

There are two kinds of proteins moving things across a membrane, and the difference between them is the point of this video.

A **channel** is a door. It opens, and whatever it carries goes whichever way the gradient already points. A channel has no direction of its own. It doesn't push. It doesn't decide. It costs nothing to use, because the gradient does all the work.

A **pump** is the opposite. A pump moves things *against* their gradient, uphill, from where there's less to where there's more. That doesn't happen on its own, so a pump has to spend energy. It burns ATP every time.

Channels release gradients. Pumps build them.

### Scene 3: Gradients are built, not found

*[Visual: sodium-potassium pump cycling, ATP being consumed]*

So gradients don't just happen. Gradients are built, on purpose, at real expense, and then held there.

How expensive? The sodium-potassium pump alone, just that one protein doing that one job, is commonly estimated to use something like a fifth of your resting energy. In your neurons it's far more than that. A significant share of everything you ate today went to keeping sodium out of your cells.

Which sounds absurd until you ask why it would be worth it.

### Scene 4: A gradient is a battery

*[Visual: gradient shown as a charged battery. Then a channel opens and the battery discharges through it, turning a wheel.]*

Here's why. A gradient is stored energy.

When you build a difference across a membrane, you've charged a battery. And then, any time the cell needs work done, it opens the right channel and lets that difference collapse in a controlled way, and the collapse does the work.

That's not a metaphor I'm using to be nice. That's genuinely how your cells make ATP, how nerves fire, how your gut absorbs nutrients, how plants capture sunlight. We'll build all of that later this semester, and every single one of them is the same shape: a membrane, a difference across it, and a door.

### Scene 5: Equilibrium is death

*[Visual: two cells side by side. One with gradients, labeled alive. One fully equalized, labeled not.]*

So let's correct something you may have been taught, or may just assume.

The body is not trying to reach balance. A cell at perfect balance with its surroundings is a cell that has nothing stored, nothing to spend, and no way to do work.

Equilibrium is death. Staying out of equilibrium is what being alive costs.

### Scene 6: Bridge

Now hold that, because we're finally ready to go back to where we started.

That toxin we introduced, produced by that tiny, unassuming bacteria? It doesn't create anything. It doesn't push anything. It doesn't move a single molecule of water.

All it does is open a door on a battery your cells already charged.

# Video 5: Cholera, Explained

**1,014 words ≈ 7.5 min** | The payoff

### Scene 1: Back to the beginning

*[Visual: return to the opening image from Video 1, the person losing water]*

Back to cholera. Why is an infected person shriveling, losing all of their water?

You now have every piece you need to answer that. Let's put them together.

### Scene 2: Where it happens

*[Visual: zoom into the small intestine, epithelial cells lining the tube, bacteria in the lumen]*

All of this happens in the small intestine.

*Vibrio cholerae* is a bacterium, comma shaped, that lives in water. You swallow it, it reaches your small intestine, and then it does something surprising. It doesn't invade. It never enters a single one of your cells. It sits in the open space of your gut, attaches to the lining, and releases a protein.

And here's something worth pausing on. The inside of your digestive tract is a tube that passes through you. What's in that tube hasn't entered your body yet. Topologically, the inside of your gut is *outside* of you. That's going to matter in about four minutes.

### Scene 3: The doorknob

*[Visual: cholera toxin approaching the cell surface, five-part base binding a sugar chain on the glycocalyx]*

The protein it releases is cholera toxin, and it's shaped like a payload sitting on a base of five identical pieces. Those five pieces have one job: to grab.

What they grab is a specific sugar sitting on the surface of your intestinal cells. Part of the glycocalyx. The sugar coat.

The name tag. I told you it was also a doorknob.

Once it's docked, your cell does what cells do with things bound to their surface. It brings it inside. The toxin gets a ride in, on a system built for something else entirely.

### Scene 4: The switch that won't turn off

*[Visual: a switch protein cycling on and off normally. Then the toxin modifies it and it locks in the on position.]*

Inside the cell, the active part of the toxin goes looking for one specific target: a protein that works as a switch.

Under normal conditions that switch turns on when a signal arrives, does its job, and then turns itself off. That automatic shutoff is built in, and it's the reason your cells can respond to a signal without responding to it forever.

The toxin chemically changes that switch so it can no longer turn itself off.

It's welded on. And everything downstream of it now runs continuously.

### Scene 5: CFTR

*[Visual: signal cascade, then a chloride channel in the apical membrane opening and staying open]*

What's downstream is a chloride channel called CFTR, sitting in the membrane facing the inside of your gut. Write that name down. You'll see it again in the inheritance unit, and it will be doing something very different.

The stuck signal opens CFTR. And it doesn't close.

Now, CFTR is a channel, not a pump. So it doesn't push chloride anywhere. It just opens a door, and chloride goes wherever it was already going to go.

Which way is that? Out. Because your intestinal cells have been actively loading chloride in from the other side, spending energy to hold more chloride inside the cell than would ever collect there on its own. They built that gradient. They charged that battery.

CFTR is the release valve.

### Scene 6: Follow the physics

*[Visual: Cl⁻ flooding out, Na⁺ following, then water pouring through aquaporins into the lumen]*

Chloride pours out of the cell into your gut. Chloride is negative, so sodium follows it out to balance the charge.

And now the inside of your intestine is loaded with salt. Far more solute out there than in the cell.

Water goes where solute is. That's osmosis, and osmosis isn't a decision anybody makes. Water moves down its own gradient because that's what water does.

*[Visual: aquaporins, wide open, water streaming through at full speed]*

And how does it get there? Through the aquaporins. Working perfectly. At full speed. Doing exactly the job they were built to do.

Look at what that means. The toxin never touched water. Not once. It opened a chloride channel and nothing else. Every water channel in that cell is functioning correctly.

The person is losing a liter an hour because their membranes are working, exactly as designed, in response to a gradient that should not exist.

### Scene 7: Already gone

*[Visual: water tracing from bloodstream, to tissue, to cell, to gut lumen]*

Remember that the inside of your gut is outside your body. So the moment that water crosses into the intestine, it's already left you. Before anything is expelled, the loss has happened.

That's why someone dies of dehydration from a problem that never leaves their gut.

### Scene 8: The fix

*[Visual: a packet of oral rehydration salts, then a glass of water]*

Now here's my favorite part of this entire story.

For most of human history, cholera was a death sentence. What changed wasn't an antibiotic. What changed was that somebody figured out you could beat it with sugar water.

Oral rehydration solution is water, salt, and glucose. That's it. It costs pennies.

*[Visual: a cotransporter on the other face of the cell, hauling Na⁺ and glucose in together]*

It works because of a different protein, one the toxin never touched. This one carries sodium and glucose across together, in the same direction, at the same time. Neither one moves well alone. Together, they move.

*[Visual: solute now higher on the cell side, water reversing back through the same aquaporins]*

And once sodium is going back into the cell, the cell side becomes the saltier side. Water follows it back in. Through the same aquaporins. Same channels, opposite direction.

You don't repair the leak. You build a counter current.

Case fatality drops from thirty or forty percent to under one percent. The Lancet once called it potentially the most important medical advance of the century, and it is, essentially, applied membrane biology.

### Scene 9: Close

*[Visual: return to the simple bilayer from Video 1]*

The membrane is what kills the patient. The membrane is also what saves them. Both times for the same reason: what crosses, and when, and through what.

And you're going to see this shape again. When we get to how your cells pull energy out of food, you'll find a membrane with a gradient across it and a channel that lets it fall. When we get to photosynthesis, same structure, different membrane. When we get to how a nerve fires, same structure again.

Gradients across membranes. That isn't a topic in this course. That's the course.
