### Scene 3: Three jobs

**☐ CAM 10** | draws: clean membrane, centered

Here's how I want you to think about all of this. A boundary has three jobs. Just three.

**☐ CAM 10** | draws: "1. MATTER," transport proteins highlight

One. Things have to be able to physically cross it. That's matter.

**☐ CAM 10** | draws: "2. INFORMATION," receptors highlight

Two. Messages have to be able to cross it. That's information.

**☐ CAM 10** | draws: "3. IDENTITY," sugar chains highlight

Three. The outside of it has to say who this cell is. That's identity.

**☐ CAM 11** | draws: all three labels held together, wide shot

Every molecule I'm about to show you is doing one of those three jobs. Which means if you forget a name, and you will forget some names, you can still ask yourself which job it's doing.

### Scene 4: Matter, the free ones

**☐ CAM 12** | draws: membrane with a line of molecules approaching from outside

Let's start with matter. Things getting across.

**☐ CAM 12** | draws: O₂ and CO₂ passing through, no protein involved *(GIF, reuse CAM 5)*

We already did the first one. If a molecule is small and it has no charge, it just goes through. Oxygen. Carbon dioxide. Nothing has to help it.

### Scene 5: Matter, channels

**☐ CAM 13** | draws: Na⁺ in its hydration sphere bouncing off the bare bilayer *(GIF)*

But sodium is charged, and that insulated center rejects it. Sodium needs a door.

**☐ CAM 14** | draws: a channel protein spanning the membrane

That door is called a channel protein. It goes all the way through the membrane, from the outside to the inside.

**☐ CAM 14** | draws: zoom on the channel lining, charges marked along the inside

And the inside of that channel is lined with charges. That gives the ion something to hold onto the whole way across.

**☐ CAM 15** | draws: Na⁺ entering, hydration sphere releasing, ion passing, water re-forming on the other side *(GIF)*

It lets go of its sphere of hydration and grabs onto the lining of the channel instead. Then it picks the water back up once it's through. It's a trade, not a sacrifice.

**☐ CAM 16** | draws: a second channel, a different ion approaching and being turned away

And channels are picky. Really picky. A channel that lets sodium through will turn other ions away, even ones that look almost the same. We'll come back to why that is.

### Scene 6: Matter, carriers

**☐ CAM 17** | draws: glucose molecule, labeled, approaching

Now here's the one that surprises people. Glucose. Sugar.

**☐ CAM 17** | draws: "NO CHARGE" label on glucose

Glucose has no charge at all. By everything I just told you, glucose should slide right through.

**☐ CAM 18** | draws: glucose bouncing off the bilayer anyway

It doesn't. Glucose is too big, and even without a full charge, it's polar. Which means it gets rejected too.

**☐ CAM 18** | draws: glucose held against a channel, not fitting

And it doesn't fit through a channel either.

**☐ CAM 19** | draws: carrier protein in three states, open-out, bound, open-in *(GIF)*

Glucose needs a different kind of protein. This one's called a carrier. A carrier grabs the molecule on one side, changes its own shape, and lets it go on the other side.

**☐ CAM 20** | draws: channel labeled "door," carrier labeled "revolving door"

A channel is a door. A carrier is more like a revolving door. Something has to physically move.

### Scene 7: Matter, pumps

**☐ CAM 21** | draws: all previous molecules with downhill arrows, "more" to "less"

Everything I've shown you so far has one thing in common. All of it is moving from where there's more of it to where there's less of it. Downhill. And it's free.

**☐ CAM 22** | draws: a molecule at the bottom of a hill, target at the top

But sometimes a cell needs to move something the wrong way. Uphill. From where there's less of it to where there's more.

**☐ CAM 22** | draws: pump protein, ATP being spent, molecule forced upward *(GIF)*

That takes a pump. And a pump costs energy, every single time it runs.

**☐ CAM 23** | draws: list, "channels / carriers / pumps"

We're going to spend a whole video on why a cell would ever pay for that. For right now, just hold onto three ways across. Channels, carriers, and pumps.

### Scene 8: Information

**☐ CAM 24** | draws: a signal molecule approaching the membrane

That's matter. Now job two. Information.

**☐ CAM 24** | draws: text, "SAME RULE"

And here's what I really want you to notice. The membrane does not have a separate set of rules for messages. Signals are molecules too, and the same rule applies to them.

**☐ CAM 25** | draws: steroid hormone, small and nonpolar, crossing straight through *(GIF)*

Some signals are small and they have no charge. Steroid hormones are lipids, remember, so they cross the membrane the same way oxygen does. They go straight in.

**☐ CAM 25** | draws: steroid binding a receptor inside the cell, then moving into the nucleus

Which means their receptor isn't in the membrane at all. It's inside the cell. Sometimes it goes all the way into the nucleus.

**☐ CAM 26** | draws: a larger charged signal molecule bouncing off the outside

But most signals are charged, or they're big, or they're polar. Those can't cross. Same reason as everything else.

**☐ CAM 27** | draws: receptor protein spanning the membrane, one end out, one end in

For those, the receptor has to sit in the membrane, with one end sticking out and one end sticking in.

**☐ CAM 27** | draws: signal binds the outside end, protein changes shape, inside end changes *(GIF)*

The signal binds to the outside end. That makes the protein change shape. And that shape change is the message.

**☐ CAM 28** | draws: signal still sitting outside, arrow labeled "message" moving inward

The signal itself never comes in. The information does. Two different solutions, and it's the same rule deciding which one you get.

### Scene 9: Identity

**☐ CAM 29** | draws: membrane, sugar chains drawing onto the outer face only

Job three. Identity. Look at the outside face of the membrane, and only the outside face.

**☐ CAM 29** | draws: labels, "glycoprotein" and "glycolipid"

**☐ CAM 30** | draws: two cells approaching, sugar coats matching

All together, that sugar coating is how cells recognize each other. It's how your immune system tells the difference between your cells and something that doesn't belong.

**☐ CAM 30** | draws: "NAME TAG" label

**☐ CAM 31** | draws: a bacterial toxin locking onto one specific sugar chain

It is also, unfortunately, a doorknob. And we are absolutely going to come back to that.
